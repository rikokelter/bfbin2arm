1 Scope

This vignette deals exclusively with the two-sided Bayes factor test

\[ H_0:p_1=p_2 \qquad \text{versus} \qquad H_1:p_1\neq p_2, \]

where \(p_1\) and \(p_2\) denote the event probabilities in the control and treatment arms. We write \(\mathrm{BF}_{01}\) for the marginal likelihood under the common-probability null divided by the marginal likelihood under the unequal-probability alternative. Small values support \(H_1\) and large values support \(H_0\).

Directional tests are covered by a separate vignette. They require additional choices about whether effects in the unexpected direction are excluded a priori or included in a composite null, as well as truncated priors and hypothesis-specific normalising constants. Keeping the examples separate makes the prior specification, decision rule, and operating characteristics of the two-sided test transparent.

2 Model and priors

Let

\[ Y_1\sim\operatorname{Bin}(n_1,p_1),\qquad Y_2\sim\operatorname{Bin}(n_2,p_2), \]

independently. Under \(H_0\), a common response probability is assigned a beta prior,

\[ p_1=p_2=p\mid H_0\sim\operatorname{Beta}(a_0,b_0). \]

Under \(H_1\), independent beta priors are assigned to the arm-specific probabilities,

\[ p_1\mid H_1\sim\operatorname{Beta}(a_1,b_1),\qquad p_2\mid H_1\sim\operatorname{Beta}(a_2,b_2). \]

The package distinguishes analysis priors, which determine the Bayes factor applied to the observed data, from design priors, which average the operating characteristics at the planning stage. The two sets of priors may be identical, but they need not be.

For beta analysis priors, the Bayes factor is

\[ \mathrm{BF}_{01}(y_1,y_2)= \frac{\mathrm{B}(a_0^a+y_1+y_2,b_0^a+n_1+n_2-y_1-y_2)} {\mathrm{B}(a_0^a,b_0^a)} \frac{\mathrm{B}(a_1^a,b_1^a)\mathrm{B}(a_2^a,b_2^a)} {\mathrm{B}(a_1^a+y_1,b_1^a+n_1-y_1) \mathrm{B}(a_2^a+y_2,b_2^a+n_2-y_2)}. \]

The function twoarmbinbf01() evaluates this expression. The functions powertwoarmbinbf01() and design_twoarm_onestage_bf() compute exact operating characteristics by summing over all possible pairs \((y_1,y_2)\); no Monte Carlo simulation is needed.

3 Evidence and calibration

For an evidence threshold \(k<1\), the efficacy region is

\[ \mathcal{R}_k=\{(y_1,y_2):\mathrm{BF}_{01}(y_1,y_2)<k\}. \]

The package reports the design-prior predictive probability of this region under \(H_1\) as Power and under \(H_0\) as Type1_Error. For a threshold \(k_f>1\), CE_H0 is the predictive probability that \(\mathrm{BF}_{01}>k_f\) under \(H_0\). Optional frequentist operating characteristics are obtained by summing over the same evidence region at fixed values of \((p_1,p_2)\).

The examples use \(k=1/10\) and \(k_f=10\) for strong evidence. Other thresholds should be prespecified and examined in sensitivity analyses.

4 Example 1: CONSIDER - a two-sided example with balanced allocation

The CONSIDER trial was a multicentre, randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled phase II trial of canakinumab in adult-onset Still’s disease (Kedor et al. 2020). Its binary primary endpoint was a clinically relevant reduction in disease activity at week 12, defined by a decrease in DAS28 greater than 1.2. Recruitment ended early. The efficacy analysis contained 17 placebo patients and 18 canakinumab patients; 7 and 12, respectively, met the primary endpoint.

4.3 Comparing calibration approaches

We now determine the required sample size prospectively using four different calibration approaches. All four examples use the same two-sided Bayes-factor test, analysis priors, design priors, evidence threshold, allocation ratio, and sample-size search range. Only the operating-characteristic constraints used to select the design differ.

The hypotheses are

\[ H_0:p_1=p_2 \qquad\text{versus}\qquad H_1:p_1\neq p_2, \]

where group 1 denotes placebo and group 2 denotes canakinumab. Evidence against \(H_0\) is declared when \(\operatorname{BF}_{01}<1/10\).

For the Bayesian operating characteristics, we retain the illustrative design priors

\[ p\mid H_0\sim\operatorname{Beta}(1,1), \]

and

\[ p_1\mid H_1\sim\operatorname{Beta}(3,9), \qquad p_2\mid H_1\sim\operatorname{Beta}(8,4), \]

independently. Although these design priors favour a treatment benefit, they are not truncated: both directions of a difference remain possible, and the hypothesis test remains two-sided. Uniform analysis priors are used under both models.

Frequentist power is evaluated at the fixed alternative \((p_1,p_2)=(0.25,2/3)\). Frequentist type-I error is evaluated over a grid of common event probabilities under \(H_0\).

Throughout this section, “frequentist calibration” refers to calibrating the frequentist operating characteristics of the same Bayes-factor decision rule, not replacing it with a conventional frequentist test.

library(bfbin2arm)

4.3.1 Frequentist calibration

The first design requires at least 80% frequentist power at the prespecified alternative and a frequentist type-I-error rate no larger than 5% over the supplied null grid.

The relevant targets are target_freq_power and target_freq_type1. Bayesian operating characteristics are also calculated, but they do not determine feasibility in this calibration.

consider_frequentist <- design_twoarm_onestage_bf(
  n_min = 10,
  n_max = 200,
  algorithm = "optimal",

  # Two-sided Bayes-factor decision rule
  test = "BF01",
  k = 1/10,
  k_f = 10,

  # Enforce frequentist power and frequentist type-I error
  calibration = "frequentist",
  target_freq_power = 0.80,
  target_freq_type1 = 0.05,

  # No additional compelling-evidence constraint
  target_ce_h0 = 0,

  # Design and analysis priors under H0
  a_0_d = 1, b_0_d = 1,
  a_0_a = 1, b_0_a = 1,

  # Design priors under H1
  a_1_d = 3, b_1_d = 9,
  a_2_d = 8, b_2_d = 4,

  # Analysis priors under H1
  a_1_a = 1, b_1_a = 1,
  a_2_a = 1, b_2_a = 1,

  # Fixed alternative for frequentist power
  p1_power = 0.25,
  p2_power = 2/3,

  # Identical grids ensure evaluation along p1 = p2
  p1_grid = seq(0, 1, by = 0.02),
  p2_grid = seq(0, 1, by = 0.02),

  # Equal allocation and sustained feasibility
  alloc1 = 0.5,
  alloc2 = 0.5,
  sustain_n = 10L,
  power_cushion = 0,

  report_freq_type1 = TRUE,
  progress = FALSE
)

4.3.4 Full calibration

The fourth design simultaneously requires:

  • Bayesian power of at least 80%.
  • Frequentist power of at least 80% at the specified alternative.
  • Bayesian type-I-error rate no larger than 5%.
  • Frequentist type-I-error rate no larger than 5% over the null grid.

All four targets are therefore supplied explicitly.

consider_full <- design_twoarm_onestage_bf(
  n_min = 10,
  n_max = 200,
  algorithm = "optimal",

  # Two-sided Bayes-factor decision rule
  test = "BF01",

  target_type1 = 0.05,

Having run each calibration separately, we see that in this case, Bayesian, hybrid and full calibration yield the same designs. This is, of course, not always the case.

4.3.6 Sustained-feasibility diagnostics

Each example uses sustain_n = 10L. Consequently, the calibration checks the relevant constraints at the candidate total sample size and over a forward window of ten additional candidate total sample sizes, within the search range.

4.3.7 Interpreting frequentist error control

The reported frequentist type-I error is the maximum over the supplied null grid. It approximates, but does not certify, the supremum over the continuous interval \(p\in[0,1]\). A finer grid or continuous maximisation should therefore be used when stricter numerical verification is required.

Likewise, the 80% frequentist power requirement applies to the specified alternative \((0.25,2/3)\), not to every possible unequal pair of event probabilities. Bayesian power instead averages over the entire alternative design prior.

The design priors should represent defensible pretrial uncertainty. They should not be made more concentrated merely to obtain a smaller required sample size.

5 Example 2: NADIM II - a two-sided example with unequal allocation

5.1 Trial setting and observed data

NADIM II was a randomized phase II trial in resectable stage IIIA/IIIB non-small-cell lung cancer. Its experimental:control allocation was 2:1 (Provencio et al. 2023). The primary binary endpoint was pathological complete response after neoadjuvant treatment. The experimental regimen added nivolumab to chemotherapy; control patients received chemotherapy alone (Provencio et al. 2023).

The reported primary-analysis counts were 21 responses among 57 experimental patients and 2 among 29 control patients. Patients who did not undergo surgery were counted as nonresponders, rather than removed from these denominators (Provencio et al. 2023). The original publication reported two-sided tests (Provencio et al. 2023).

Here and throughout the code, arm 1 is control and arm 2 is experimental. Thus the trial’s 2:1 experimental:control allocation corresponds to alloc1 = 1/3 and alloc2 = 2/3.

5.2 Two-sided Bayes-factor analysis

We compare

\[ H_0:p_1=p_2 \qquad\text{versus}\qquad H_1:p_1\neq p_2. \]

The analysis priors are \(p\mid H_0\sim\operatorname{Beta}(1,1)\) and, independently, \(p_1,p_2\mid H_1\sim\operatorname{Beta}(1,1)\). Evidence against \(H_0\) is declared when \(\operatorname{BF}_{01}<0.1\). This is a trial-inspired Bayesian reanalysis, not a reconstruction of the original frequentist test.

bf01_nadim <- twoarmbinbf01(
  y1 = y1_nadim, y2 = y2_nadim,
  n1 = n1_nadim, n2 = n2_nadim,
  a_0_a = 1, b_0_a = 1,
  a_1_a = 1, b_1_a = 1,
  a_2_a = 1, b_2_a = 1
)

# Independent log-scale check; binomial coefficients cancel.
log_bf01_nadim <-
  lbeta(1 + y1_nadim + y2_nadim,
        1 + n1_nadim + n2_nadim - y1_nadim - y2_nadim) -
  lbeta(1 + y1_nadim, 1 + n1_nadim - y1_nadim) -
  lbeta(1 + y2_nadim, 1 + n2_nadim - y2_nadim)

stopifnot(
  is.finite(bf01_nadim),
  isTRUE(all.equal(bf01_nadim, exp(log_bf01_nadim), tolerance = 1e-10))
)

c(
  BF01 = bf01_nadim,
  BF10 = 1 / bf01_nadim,
  risk_difference = y2_nadim / n2_nadim - y1_nadim / n1_nadim
)

The resulting \(\operatorname{BF}_{01}\) is approximately 0.0366, corresponding to \(\operatorname{BF}_{10}\) of approximately 27.3. The Bayes factor supports unequal response probabilities. The observed rates indicate which arm has the higher response rate; a two-sided Bayes factor alone is not a directional test.

5.3 Prospective planning assumptions

The original trial’s sample-size calculation assumed response probabilities of 0.10 under control and 0.40 under experimental treatment, 80% power, 5% alpha, and a planned sample of 90 allowing for 15% dropout (Provencio et al. 2023). We use those planning probabilities, not the observed response rates, to construct a prospective-design illustration.

For frequentist power, the fixed alternative is \((p_1,p_2)=(0.10,0.40)\). For Bayesian power we use independent design priors

\[ p_1\mid H_1\sim\operatorname{Beta}(2,18), \qquad p_2\mid H_1\sim\operatorname{Beta}(8,12). \]

Their means are 0.10 and 0.40 and their beta concentration parameters \(a+b\) are both 20. The concentration 20 is an illustrative choice for this vignette, not a prior specification from the NADIM II protocol. It should be varied in sensitivity analyses. The priors are not truncated to \(p_2>p_1\); the test remains two-sided.

Under \(H_0\), the common response probability has the uniform design prior \(p\sim\operatorname{Beta}(1,1)\). This is also an illustrative choice, retained for comparability with the preceding example; it is not inferred from the pooled NADIM II data. Analysis priors remain uniform in every calibration. Consequently, design-prior information enters predictive operating characteristics, not the Bayes factor as borrowed historical observations.

All four calibrations below use \(k=0.1\), a fixed 1:2 control:experimental allocation, and a search over total sample sizes from 12 to 240. The ceiling is a bounded teaching example, not a claim that all possible prior specifications are feasible within this range. With sustain_n = 10L, the relevant constraints must hold at the candidate total and at the next ten candidate totals, subject to the search range. The package documents these arguments and the four calibration modes in its reference manual.

All selected sample sizes are totals across both arms. Arm sizes are rounded to integers, so the resulting ratio need not be exactly 1:2. The package conditions its calculations on the selected arm sizes; it does not additionally average over random allocation-count imbalance. If exact 1:2 counts are required, a suitable total divisible by three must be chosen and its operating characteristics re-evaluated.

A design may satisfy frequentist power at \((0.10,0.40)\) before it satisfies Bayesian power, since the latter averages over uncertain response probabilities, including smaller differences. Calibration modes can therefore select different totals. Conversely, several modes may select the same total when their additional constraints are already satisfied. No sample-size saving is presumed for Bayesian calibration.

The frequentist type-I error shown is a maximum over the supplied grid, not a certified supremum over the continuous null space. A finer grid or continuous maximisation is needed for stricter numerical verification. The 80% frequentist power target is pointwise at the specified alternative. PCE_H0 is reported but not constrained.

These totals concern participants with binary outcomes defined according to the analysis strategy. They are not directly comparable to the original planned recruitment target of 90 without considering its dropout allowance, different testing rule, and outcome-handling assumptions. Failure to undergo surgery is an endpoint failure in this illustration, not automatically a missing outcome warranting dropout inflation.

5.10 Joint sensitivity to the evidence threshold, design-prior concentration, and null-evidence requirement

We now investigate how the selected sample size depends on the evidence threshold, uncertainty in the alternative design prior, and an additional requirement on the probability of obtaining compelling evidence for the null hypothesis.

This sensitivity analysis varies the concentration of the alternative design priors to describe the consequences of different planning assumptions. It does not justify choosing a more concentrated prior merely to obtain a smaller sample size.

The alternative design priors have the common form

\[ p_1\mid H_1\sim\operatorname{Beta}(0.10s,0.90s), \qquad p_2\mid H_1\sim\operatorname{Beta}(0.40s,0.60s). \]

Their means remain 0.10 and 0.40, while the concentration \(s=a+b\) controls their dispersion. We examine all six combinations of

\[ k\in\left\{\frac{1}{10},\frac{1}{3}\right\} \qquad\text{and}\qquad s\in\{20,50,100\}. \]

Uniform analysis priors are retained in every scenario. Consequently, changing \(s\) changes predictive operating characteristics but does not change the Bayes-factor decision rule at a fixed sample size. In particular, it does not change frequentist power or frequentist type-I error at a fixed design.

The null design prior remains

\[ p\mid H_0\sim\operatorname{Beta}(1,1). \]

All scenarios retain 1:2 control-to-experimental allocation, frequentist power evaluation at \((p_1,p_2)=(0.10,0.40)\), and the same 80% power targets and 5% type-I-error bounds.

For each scenario, we perform frequentist, hybrid, and full calibration twice:

  • Without an additional probability-of-compelling-evidence requirement.
  • With the additional requirement

\[ \operatorname{PCE}(H_0) = P\!\left(\operatorname{BF}_{01}>10\mid H_0\right) \geq 0.60. \]

The threshold k_f = 3 is held fixed throughout. Thus, the second analysis requires a 60% predictive probability of obtaining at least threefold evidence for equality under the null design prior.

This requirement is distinct from type-I-error control: avoiding false positive conclusions does not imply that the trial frequently produces strong evidence for equality.

5.10.3 Extracting results and checking the forward window

  • A feasible design with a complete ten-step forward window.
  • No feasible design found within the search range.

For the last outcome, the tables request an extended search rather than presenting the selected total as fully verified.

      k = run$scenario$evidence_threshold,

      Calibration = mode,
      PCE_target = run$pce_target,
      Status = status,
      N_total = selected_n,
      n_control = NA_real_,
      n_experimental = NA_real_,
      Bayesian_power = NA_real_,
      Bayesian_T1E = NA_real_,
      Frequentist_power = NA_real_,
      Frequentist_T1E_grid = NA_real_,
      PCE_H0 = NA_real_,
      stringsAsFactors = FALSE
    )

    if (package_feasible &&
        !is.null(x$operating_characteristics)) {
      oc <- x$operating_characteristics

      out$n_control <- unname(x$design["n1"])
      out$n_experimental <- unname(x$design["n2"])

      out$Bayesian_T1E <- oc$type1
      out$Frequentist_power <- oc$freq_power
      out$Frequentist_T1E_grid <- oc$freq_type1
      out$PCE_H0 <- oc$ce_h0
    }

    nadim_sensitivity_rows[[length(nadim_sensitivity_rows) + 1L]] <- out
  }

5.10.7 Interpretation

Without an additional PCE requirement, increasing the alternative design-prior concentration can reduce the sample sizes required by hybrid and full calibration. This occurs because the prior puts less probability on response configurations with small treatment differences.

It does not reduce the frequentist calibration size: the analysis priors, fixed frequentist alternative, evidence threshold, and allocation are unchanged. Consequently, within a given evidence threshold, the frequentist sample-size result should be identical across the three alternative design-prior concentrations.

Relaxing the alternative-evidence threshold from \(k=1/10\) to \(k=1/3\) changes the decision rule. It increases the probability of crossing the evidence threshold under both the alternative and the null. The resulting sample-size reduction must therefore be interpreted together with the recalculated type-I-error probabilities.

The PCE requirement behaves differently. Under a fixed null design prior, fixed analysis priors, fixed allocation, and fixed k_f, its probability does not depend on the alternative design-prior concentration. It also does not depend on the alternative-evidence threshold k.

Thus, although changing k or the concentration may reduce the power-driven sample-size requirement, neither change directly improves the probability of obtaining \(\operatorname{BF}_{01}>10\) under \(H_0\) at a given sample size.

If the null-evidence requirement becomes the limiting constraint, different calibration modes and prior-concentration scenarios may select the same sample size. Alternatively, no feasible design may be found within the bounded search range.

Such a result is informative: a trial capable of detecting a treatment difference need not also be capable of frequently producing strong evidence for equality.

If further searches are warranted, increase the common search ceiling and rerun the analysis after confirming numerical stability.

The prior concentrations examined here are sensitivity assumptions, not alternative specifications to be selected solely according to which produces the smallest sample size. Their substantive plausibility should be assessed using defensible pretrial information.

All sample sizes are totals across both arms unless labelled otherwise. The selected integer arm sizes approximate the requested allocation ratio. All frequentist type-I-error results are maxima over the supplied finite null grid; a finer grid or continuous maximisation is needed for stricter verification of a final proposed design.

6 Summary

A two-sided Bayes-factor design answers whether the treatment and control event probabilities differ without building a preferred direction into the alternative. The observed direction must therefore be reported using the arm-specific event rates or an effect estimate. The bfbin2arm workflow consists of:

  1. selecting the two-sided test with test = "BF01";
  2. specifying separate design and analysis priors under \(H_0\) and \(H_1\);
  3. choosing evidence thresholds \(k\) and \(k_f\);
  4. evaluating the exact predictive and optional frequentist operating characteristics; and
  5. searching for the smallest design that satisfies prespecified calibration constraints.

The small CONSIDER example illustrates weak evidence after early termination, whereas the NADIM example illustrates two-sided evidence in a different clinical domain under unequal allocation.
